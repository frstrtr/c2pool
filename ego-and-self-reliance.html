Ego and Self-Reliance. A Plain-Language Handbook

frstrtr — 2026

What this is about

Every day we make decisions: answer or stay silent, take on a job or turn it down, help or not help, leave or stay. Some of these decisions are made not by us but by a fast reaction that watches one thing only: how we look to other people and what they will think of us. This reaction fires before we have time to think, and it is very good at looking like common sense.

This handbook is about how to recognize such reactions before they turn into actions. It has five parts: what the ego is and how it works; which other voices take part in our decisions and how to use them; the mistakes they make most often; what self-reliance is; and a step-by-step check for any decision, with what to do afterwards.

For clarity, the same example runs through the whole text: Guy Ritchie's film Revolver (2005). The main character is Jake Green. The figure the whole plot is built around is Sam Gold, a man everyone fears and no one has ever seen. The two people who lead Jake against Gold are Avi and Zach; in the reading the film points to, they also exist only inside Jake's head. Passages from the film are marked "From Revolver." You do not have to read them; the handbook is complete without them. The film's history, its source, and the limits of its use are described in the appendix at the end.

1. What the ego is

A simple definition

In this handbook, the ego means a set of fast, automatic reactions that guard two things:

  • our place among people (not to end up unwanted, weak, unnecessary);
  • our picture of ourselves (to remain, in our own eyes, smart, strong, good).

Where it came from

For a very long time a human being could not survive alone. Losing your place in your group meant being left without food, protection, and help. So the reactions that watch our standing among people are very old, very fast, and very insistent. They have not gone anywhere, even though today losing the respect of acquaintances rarely threatens anyone's life.

How it works

These reactions have several properties worth knowing.

They are fast. They fire before we have thought. Resentment, shame, the urge to prove something appear in a fraction of a second.

They do not distinguish a real threat from a threat to reputation. To them, "I will be fired" and "they will think badly of me" are the same thing.

They do not understand situations in which other people's opinion does not matter. They will defend our standing even where there is no standing at all, for example in an argument with a stranger on the internet.

They know how to look like reasonable arguments. That is exactly why they are hard to notice.

What the ego is not

The ego is not an enemy and not a separate creature living inside. It is a part of ourselves with a job of its own: to keep us from losing people. Fighting it is useless and unnecessary. The task is different: to notice when it has switched on, and not to give it the last word.

The analogy: Sam Gold

In Revolver everyone is afraid of Sam Gold. He is the most powerful man in the city: people pay him, report to him, and threaten others with his name. No one has ever seen him. By the end of the film it turns out there is no Gold. There is a voice in the head of the main character, Jake Green. This voice speaks in his name, promises protection, and demands fear and obedience in return. The film ends with the line that Gold's greatest trick is making you believe that he is you.

This analogy describes several properties of the ego precisely.

He is invisible because he looks out from the same place you do. People search for Gold outside, among bosses, competitors, enemies. He is in the last place anyone would think to look.

He speaks in your voice. His arguments sound like your own thoughts: "they will cheat you," "you can't let this stand," "you are smarter than them." You cannot tell his words from yours by how they sound, only by what they demand.

He calls himself your protector. In the elevator scene the voice tells Jake: I have always looked after you, without me you are nothing, you need me. This is the second tactic from section 2, being needed, applied by the ego to its own owner.

He lives on what he is fed. Every argument won, every superiority proven, every awkwardness avoided feeds him. The more he gets, the louder he speaks.

He cannot bear pain and humiliation. That is why Avi and Zach, the ones who take Jake on against Gold, make him do what the voice forbids: give away his money, endure humiliation, submit to someone else's rules. The voice has no defense against an action; it can only persuade.

Who Avi and Zach are. In the reading the film itself leads to, they do not exist either. In prison Jake communicated with them for seven years only through notes passed through the wall and never saw their faces. They vanished from prison in a way no one could explain, and they enter and leave his life after prison just as inexplicably. They work with the same lever as Gold, fear (a fake illness, three days to live), only aimed against Gold. So both the opponent and the means against him are inside one head. For this handbook that is the main thing in the film: checking a decision is not an outside judge. It is the same head asking itself questions by rules written down in advance. The "formula" Jake received through the wall in the quiet years is the checklist from section 10: something written in a calm state to be used in an uncalm one.

Where the analogy parts ways with this handbook. The film calls Gold an enemy who must be destroyed. Here the ego is treated as a part of yourself that has a job. But the film's resolution is really about the same thing: in the elevator Jake does not kill the voice and does not beat it in an argument. He stops carrying out its orders. The voice remains; what changes is who makes the decision.

Where this idea in the film comes from, what the director himself said about it, and what is worth taking from its source and what is not, is described in the appendix.

What it looks like in life

Some familiar states with the ego behind them:

  • resentment when you were not appreciated or noticed;
  • a strong urge to prove you are right even when the argument no longer decides anything;
  • shame about "what people will think" although nothing bad has happened;
  • relief or quiet pleasure when someone else is doing worse than you;
  • the pull to take on other people's troubles even when no one asked;
  • the inability to say no;
  • anxiety before a decision that is tied not to the decision itself but to how it will look.

2. The two ways the ego defends its place

The ego has only two tactics. Everything else is a variation of them.

The first tactic: being on top

To control, dictate terms, win, be right, be in charge. The feeling of safety comes from others being below.

What it looks like: arguing to win rather than to find the answer; insisting on your point after you already understand you were wrong; interrupting; making decisions for others "because I know better"; irritation when someone did something well without you.

The second tactic: being needed

To become so necessary that you will not be abandoned. To take on other people's responsibility, be convenient, rescue (solve problems for others that they did not ask you to solve), never refuse. The feeling of safety comes from others being unable to do without you.

What it looks like: doing a colleague's work "so as not to let anyone down"; teaching no one your craft so no one can replace you; agreeing when you want to refuse; feeling obliged to fix other people's lives; taking offense when your help was not appreciated.

What they have in common

Neither tactic works without another person watching. The first needs someone who has been outdone. The second needs someone who cannot do without you. Remove the audience and both tactics lose their meaning.

Hence the main question of this whole handbook: what remains of my decision if the audience is removed?

Why they are hard to notice

Both tactics look like good qualities. The first looks like leadership, decisiveness, principle. The second looks like responsibility, care, reliability. By appearance they cannot be told apart from the real qualities. There is only one way to tell: remove the audience and see what is left.

From Revolver. Dorothy Macha, the casino owner, is the first tactic in its pure form. His whole position rests on being feared. He himself, meanwhile, is paralyzed by fear of Gold, whom he has never seen, that is, of his own ego. In the final scene Macha puts a gun to Jake's head and demands that he be afraid. Jake is not afraid and walks out. Macha is left shouting "fear me" at an empty room. This is the first tactic with the audience removed: without someone who is afraid, nothing remains of "being on top."

The second tactic is shown in the film from the inside: the voice itself uses it. "I have always protected you, without me you are lost" is said not by a person who wants to be needed but by an ego that wants to stay in power. The argument is the same.

3. The other voices: alter egos

One person, several voices

A person behaves differently at work, with parents, with a child, in a quarrel, when tired. Each of these states has its own goals, its own fears, its own words, and its own "I." This is not an illness and not a weakness; everyone is built this way. In a calm state the voices are heard one at a time and quietly. Under pressure one of them takes the floor and speaks for the whole person.

Gold from section 1 is only one of these voices, the loudest. There are others, and some decisions are made by them.

In psychology this idea appears in several forms: "inner parent, adult and child," "subpersonalities," "parts." The names differ, the substance is the same: inside a person there are several stable roles, each with its own task, and they do not always agree with each other.

The main property: each one says "I"

No voice introduces itself. The Frightened One does not say "fear is speaking now"; it says "I can't." The Judge does not say "this is an assessment absorbed in childhood"; it says "I am worthless." Every voice uses Gold's trick: it makes you believe that it is the whole of you.

The consequence: until a voice is named, you cannot argue with it. The argument is with yourself, and it can be neither won nor lost. Naming the voice is the only way to get the distance at which a decision becomes possible.

Who usually speaks

These are names for recurring patterns of thought and motivation, not separate people inside you.

The list is neither complete nor scientific. These are the most common voices, found in almost everyone. For each one: what it protects against, how it sounds, which mistake from section 4 it leads to, and what its useful side is.

Gold, the keeper of your place. Protects your standing among people and your image of yourself. Sounds like: "fear me," "they will cheat you," "you can't let this stand," "you are smarter than them." Leads to war, to indispensability, to acting out of spite. Useful side: he notices real threats to your position, when they are real. Described in sections 1 and 2.

The Frightened One. The oldest voice: it appeared when you were small and really could not cope alone. Protects against being abandoned and against danger. Sounds like: "they will leave us," "everything will collapse," "it has to be now," "better to agree." Leads to surrender under pressure (step 5) and to trying to be needed at any cost. Its peculiarity is that it does not know you have grown up. It speaks from a time when you had no money, no skills, and no choice. Useful side: it is the first to notice risk.

The Judge. Often a word-for-word copy of a real person's voice: a parent, a teacher, a first boss. Protects against shame by striking first: if you have already condemned yourself, someone else's condemnation hurts less. Sounds like: "there you go again," "normal people would have long since," "impostor," "who are you kidding." Leads to comparing upward (mistake 2). Useful side: without it there are no standards of quality.

The Rescuer. Protects against guilt and against feeling unneeded. Sounds like: "they won't manage without me," "I can't refuse," "I have to," "who if not me." Leads to taking on what is not yours (mistake 1) and to indispensability (mistake 3). Useful side: it is also the one that notices who really needs help.

The Rebel. Protects freedom and dignity. Sounds like: "out of spite," "I won't, because I was told to," "let them see," "I owe no one anything." Leads to acting out of spite (mistake 4) and to what section 5 calls "going against everyone." Useful side: it is the only one that refuses bad terms.

The Craftsman. The voice of the one who does the work for the work's sake: assembles, repairs, builds, trains, teaches. It hardly speaks; it shows in what gets done. It is what remains when the audience is removed (step 1). Its weakness is that the other voices use its results. Gold puts them on display, the Rescuer turns them into indispensability, the Judge finds them insufficient.

The Observer. The one who can listen to all the others without agreeing with any of them right away. Does not hurry, counts, asks questions instead of making statements. This is the one who runs the check in section 6. Its weakness is that it is quiet and slow, and easy to imitate. The Judge can say "I'm just looking at things soberly"; Gold can say "I'm above this already." The real Observer is recognized by two signs: it is in no hurry, and it is willing to hear an objection (step 5).

How to understand these mechanisms

One voice speaks, the whole person answers for it. A decision made by the Frightened One at eleven at night is carried out the next day by the whole person, including the Craftsman, who took no part in it. So what matters is not which voice is right but which voice made the decision.

Every voice has a reason of its own, and it is usually a real one. The Frightened One fears abandonment; the Judge fears shame; the Rescuer fears guilt; the Rebel fears humiliation; Gold fears losing his place. What is outdated is not the fear but the method of protection: it was taken from the time when the voice appeared, and it does not account for what you have now.

Every voice has its conditions. Tiredness, hunger, alcohol, threat, certain people, certain places (your parents' house), certain times of day hand the floor to a certain voice. These conditions are individual, and they repeat. Knowing them, you can know in advance who will be speaking.

Voices form alliances. Gold and the Judge together produce "prove it at any cost." The Rescuer together with the Frightened One produce "I can't refuse." The Rebel together with Gold produce revenge that looks like achievement. An alliance of two voices sounds more convincing than either alone, and it is harder to recognize.

Voices argue with each other. "I can't decide" almost always means two voices want different things. The Judge demands you quit, the Craftsman wants to continue; the Rescuer calls you to help, the Rebel refuses. Willpower does not help here; it only hands the floor to one of the two.

Some voices are copies of living people. The audience mentioned in step 1 is often not outside but inside: an absorbed image of a person who once judged you. That person may be long gone from your life, or no longer alive, and the voice keeps judging. The question "if no one ever finds out" removes this audience too.

Voices do not disappear. They can be named, heard, disobeyed, but not deleted. The goal is not to be left with one voice but to have the decision made by the one who hears them all.

How to use this

  1. Recognize by the words. Each voice has its phrases (the list above). The phrase is recognized before the feeling, and before the action.
  1. Name it in the third person. Not "I am worthless" but "that is the Judge." Not "I can't refuse" but "the Rescuer is speaking." This is the same "name it" step from section 7, applied to the voice rather than to the mistake.
  1. Ask what it is protecting against, and answer with facts. "They will leave us" is answered by what will actually happen (step 0) and by what an adult has now: money, skills, other people. "There you go again" is answered by what was done and what was within my power (mistake 1). A voice calms down not from argument but from an answer to its reason.
  1. Neither argue nor obey. Arguing with a voice is a conversation between two voices; it has no end. Obeying is handing the decision to one who sees only a part. The third way: hear it, take it into account, decide as the Observer.
  1. Give each voice its job, at its time. Voices are useful when they occupy their own place and harmful when they occupy someone else's. The Frightened One draws up the list of risks but does not make the decision. The Judge checks the work after it is done, not before it is begun. The Rebel negotiates the terms but does not break off the relationship. The Rescuer helps within the limits the Observer has calculated. The Craftsman gets protected time during which the others do not speak. The Observer gets the decision.
  1. Before a hard decision, run a written poll. One line from each voice: what the Frightened One says, what the Judge says, the Rescuer, the Rebel, the Craftsman. Then the Observer's line: what the task is, what it costs, by when. The decision is made by the one who wrote all the lines. A sample is in example 5.
  1. Know your switching conditions. Who speaks in me after midnight, after a quarrel, after a conversation with a particular person. Under these conditions nothing irreversible is done; everything is postponed until the Observer (section 7, item 2).
  1. Do not hide behind the voices. "That wasn't me, that was my Rebel" is neither an explanation of an action nor an excuse. The voices are mine, and what is done on their word is mine too. Naming a voice reduces its power over the next decision, not the responsibility for the past.

From Revolver: Jake's voices

If you watch the film as the story of one person, the characters around Jake are his voices.

Gold: the keeper of the place, described above.

Avi, the chess player, is the Observer: he sees the game several moves ahead, is in no hurry, explains the rules, and gives no advice beyond the rules.

Zach, the con man, is the one who knows the tricks from the inside. In a person this is the part that has already seen its own tricks and recognizes them by repetition; in this handbook it corresponds to the written record (section 7, item 5).

Macha is not a voice but what a person turns into when Gold is left alone and talks without pause. A warning, not a part.

Sorter, the executor, is the Craftsman: he does the work without a wasted word and flawlessly, and at the decisive moment refuses to carry out an order, because there is something that matters more to him than the rules of the game.

Jake: the one who chooses. Until the elevator, they all take turns controlling him: Gold leads him into the casino, Avi and Zach use fear to make him do the opposite. In the elevator, for the first time, someone appears who can say to the voice "you are not me." From that moment the voices remain, but the decision is his.

4. The four most common mistakes

Mistake 1. Taking on what is not yours, or disowning what is

The situation. Someone close to you, or a business partner, is going through a hard time. The relationship is falling apart, the shared business is not going anywhere, the person is making decisions we do not like.

Two extremes are possible here, and the ego produces both.

"It's all because of me." It seems that if I had acted differently, said it differently, agreed to different terms, everything would have been fine. The thought is unpleasant, but it gives the feeling that everything is under control, and the ego values that more than peace. In reality other people's decisions and feelings are not in our power. Accepting blame can be easier than admitting that here we are powerless.

"It has nothing to do with me." Removing all responsibility from yourself. This is also a defense: this way you do not have to look at your real mistakes.

What to do. Divide it into two parts.

Mine: the concrete actions and decisions that were within my power. What I did, what I failed to do, what I said. This is acknowledged in full, without inflation and without excuses.

Not mine: the other person's decisions, feelings, and state. This is not taken on.

Example. A quarrel with a friend. Mine: I was an hour late and answered a remark sharply. Not mine: he decided not to call for a month. The first I can fix and take into account for the future. The second remains his choice.

From Revolver. Jake comes out after seven years in solitary with one thought: Macha is to blame. The voice in his head always knows who is guilty, and the guilty one is always outside. This is the second half of the mistake, "it has nothing to do with me." As long as someone else is guilty, there is no need to look at your own decisions, and the war with the guilty one keeps the voice busy for years ahead. The division by this handbook would look like this: mine is what I did then and what I do now; not mine is what Macha did.

Mistake 2. Comparing yourself with the wrong people

The ego chooses whom to compare us with. And it chooses not the one who will give an accurate assessment but the one who will give the desired feeling.

Comparing downward. Comparing yourself with those who are clearly weaker: former partners who never achieved anything; acquaintances whose lives are a mess; those who quit halfway. This gives a pleasant feeling of superiority. The price: your own bar drops. A victory where you cannot lose says nothing about how well I am working.

Comparing upward. Comparing yourself with what people put on display: successes on social media, job titles, stories about income. When superiority cannot be proven, the ego changes direction: what others have becomes ideal, what you have becomes worthless, and the feeling appears that "I am an impostor and will soon be found out." This feeling keeps you in constant tension but produces nothing. The goal here is not to understand the truth but to stay inside the experience: the experience creates busyness and a sense that something important is happening, without a single action.

What they have in common. Both produce an emotion instead of an assessment.

What to do. Compare with only two things: what the task requires, and yourself in the past. Other people are useful only as a source of information about how things can be done differently, not as a measure of what I am worth.

Example. I am learning the guitar. Bad comparison: the neighbor who quit after a month (downward), or the musician in a video with a million views (upward). Useful: a year ago I could not play a single song, now I play five. And separately: what does the musician in the video do that I could adopt.

From Revolver. The film opens with a rule: the only way to get smarter is to play a smarter opponent. Jake's win against Macha is built the other way round: the game is set up so that he cannot lose. It gives superiority but teaches nothing and says nothing about Jake. This is comparing downward in its pure form: there is a feeling, there is no assessment.

Mistake 3. Being indispensable

"Without me everything will fall apart." At work, in the family, among friends, in a shared venture. It looks like responsibility and devotion. It works as a way to guarantee you will not be abandoned: if it cannot be done without me, then I will not be left.

Signs:

  • I do not want to hand tasks to others even when there is someone to hand them to;
  • nowhere is it written down how my work is done, and there is no one who could do it;
  • it irritates me when something works without my participation;
  • quiet satisfaction when something breaks without me.

The check. If I am gone for a month starting tomorrow, what happens? If nothing irreparable, the work is set up to last. If catastrophe, then either this is a temporary stage with a known end date, or the indispensability is something I need myself, to hold on to people.

What to do. Describe your work so that another person could do it. Hand over at least one task and do not interfere.

From Revolver. The words of the voice in the elevator, "without me you are nothing," and the words of a person who has made himself indispensable coincide word for word. Not "the work needs me" but "without me everything will fall apart." The film shows who really benefits from indispensability: the one who declares it. It holds not the work but power over the one who believed in it.

Mistake 4. Acting out of spite, or to prove something

A decision that is about the work in words but is in fact addressed to a specific person: to show an ex, to prove something to a father, so that a colleague sees, so that a boss regrets. There may be no audience at all. But the addressee lives in your head, and it is he who sets the direction.

The peculiarity of this mistake is that such decisions often look like big achievements: starting a business, losing weight, moving away, getting a position. The problem is not the achievement. The problem is that the direction is set by another person, and when that person stops mattering, the achievement may turn out to be unnecessary.

The check. If this person disappeared from my life, would I still do it? If the decision loses its meaning, it was not mine.

From Revolver. The first thing Jake does after prison is go to Macha's casino, win, and humiliate him in front of everyone. Without Macha there would be no such game: it is addressed to him. The result: a contract on Jake's life. A decision made out of spite led to exactly what the ego promised to protect against. The whole rest of the film is spent dealing with the consequences of this one act.

5. What self-reliance is

What it is not

Self-reliance (also called autonomy) is often misunderstood. Two false pictures.

Leaving everyone. A person trusts no one, does everything alone, depends on no one. In reality he depends on his solitude: any need to ask for help is a threat to him.

Going against everyone. A person does the opposite of whatever he is told. He thinks he is free, but his direction is still set by others, only in reverse. Tell him "go right" and he goes left, and that is not his choice.

What it really is

Self-reliance is a position in which no single tie is the only support. No one person, no one employer, no one client, no one circle of friends, no one source of income carries everything: neither survival nor self-esteem.

There can be many ties. They can be important, close, dear. What matters is that if one of them breaks, the rest keeps working. It will hurt, but it will not be a catastrophe.

What it looks like in life: several sources of income rather than one; several social circles rather than one; skills that more than one employer needs; savings that will last through a search; people you can turn to besides the one closest person.

From Revolver. Avi and Zach make Jake give away all his money, to the last penny. This is an extreme the film needs in order to show the idea in its pure form. In life the task is not to own nothing but to own nothing whose loss equals losing yourself. Jake in the final scene is calm in front of the gun not because he has stopped caring but because the thing Macha could hold him with has disappeared: fear for his place.

From Revolver, the hot tub scene. Jake is running from Macha's men and calls Avi and Zach. They are sitting in a hot tub and answer: you're on your own now. The scene has several layers.

The end of guardianship. Until this moment Avi and Zach decided everything for Jake: they took his money and told him what to do and where to go. This was the same dependence as on Gold, only pointed the other way. "You're on your own now" cancels that one too. Had they come to rescue him, they would have fixed Jake in the role of the rescued, that is, done what the second tactic from section 2 does. Refusing to rescue is the last lesson.

The hot tub. Jake is being called from a place where no one is in a hurry. This is not indifference; it is the Observer's position. For Jake the threat is deadly because Gold is the one assessing it. For Avi and Zach there is nothing in it worth getting out of the water for: they know the opponent is not Macha but the voice. Their calm is the message. Panic runs; the Observer sits.

The rules are known; only the person himself can apply them. If Avi and Zach are parts of Jake, the scene describes the moment when the formula has been learned, and under pressure no one inside can take the step in place of the one who chooses. The chess master explains the rules; you sit down at the board alone. That is why the elevator comes right after this scene: the helpers have stepped back, and Jake is left to meet the voice without intermediaries.

Self-reliance taken to its conclusion. No single tie should carry everything, including the one that taught you. A method that works only while the teacher is nearby is not a method but an attachment. Here too is the difference between the film and its source, described in the appendix: an organization never says "you're on your own now"; it has courses, mentors, and membership without end. The film says it outright, and at the most dangerous moment.

The exception

There are obligations a person does not intend to leave, for example children. The rule "I can leave without catastrophe" does not apply to them. Self-reliance here means something else: such obligations are fulfilled by your own decision, not out of fear of losing your place or approval.

What I am responsible for

It is easy to go wrong here in both directions.

Believing that everything depends on me is the same mistake 1: an attempt to control what cannot be controlled. Believing that nothing depends on me is a refusal of my part.

The precise formula is this. For my actions and decisions, I am responsible. For other people's decisions and feelings, I am not. Circumstances I take into account, but I do not treat them as a verdict.

How to treat reputation

"How it looks from the outside" is not a trifle. At work, in business, in any shared venture, real things depend on reputation: trust, orders, invitations. A self-reliant person is not indifferent to reputation. The difference is where it stands.

For the ego, reputation serves as the measure of what I am worth. For a self-reliant person it is one of the things he manages, like time or money.

A simple check. If the loss of reputation is real, it has a concrete price: a client who will leave; a contract that will not be signed; an invitation that will not come. If the price cannot be named, this is not about reputation but about pride.

6. Step-by-step check of a decision

When to use it

Not for every decision. The check is needed when at least one of these signs is present:

  • anxiety that does not pass;
  • strong reluctance to do something without a clear reason;
  • the same question keeps coming back;
  • haste: it feels as if it must be decided right now;
  • a strong urge to prove, show, or answer something.

Step 0. Is there a real loss?

The question: what concretely will happen if I do or do not do this?

Name it concretely: money, deadlines, health, obligations to specific people.

If the loss is concrete, this is an ordinary task. It is solved as a task: count, plan, do. No further checking is needed.

If the whole loss comes down to "how it will look" or "what they will think of me," go on to the next steps.

Why this step comes first. Anxiety is not always about pride. Sometimes it warns of real danger: money, deadlines, health. If every anxiety is written off to the ego, a real warning can be missed.

From Revolver. Avi and Zach tell Jake he has a rare disease and three days to live. It is a lie, and it works: the ego cannot check whether a threat is real; it responds to any threat. Note that Avi and Zach use the same lever as Gold: fear is the only thing the ego understands. Step 0 is done deliberately precisely because it does not happen on its own.

Step 1. Remove the audience

The question: if no one would ever find out about this decision, act, or result, would I still do it?

What is being checked: whether I need the result itself or the reaction to it.

An important clarification. Things that involve other people (family, work, a shared venture) do not become "ego" just because people are involved. The question is not whether there are people but what the thing is done for: for the result, or for how the result will be noticed.

Example. A morning run. If I run even when there is no one to tell about it, it is mine. If without a photo on social media the run loses its point, it is about the audience.

Sign of the ego: the value disappears with the audience. Sign of self-reliance: the result is needed for its own sake.

From Revolver. "Fear me," said to someone who is not afraid, is exactly a decision with the audience removed. The final scene shows what remains of the first tactic without a public: nothing. Macha has not become weaker; the one everything was designed for has simply disappeared.

Step 2. Remove the addressee

The question: if the person I am mentally answering with this act disappeared from my life, would I still do it?

What it catches: spite, proving, revenge, rescuing (mistakes 1 and 4). There may be no audience, but there is an addressee.

Example. Starting a business. If I take my former boss out of my head and the idea still appeals, it is mine. If the main thing I picture is his face at the moment he finds out, he is making the decision.

Sign of the ego: without the addressee the decision loses its meaning. Sign of self-reliance: the decision does not change.

From Revolver. Jake's game at Macha's casino. Take Macha away and Jake has nothing to do there. The decision was made by the addressee. A full step-by-step breakdown of this act is in example 4.

Step 3. What am I comparing with?

The question: by what do I judge the result?

Bad measures: other people's reaction, someone else's success, "how it looks from the outside." Good measures: what the task requires, and what I had before.

An additional question: who am I comparing myself with right now, and why with them? (Mistake 2.)

Step 4. What do I want influence for?

The question: is the power or influence I want needed for what it lets me do, or for what it says about my place among people?

An additional question: does my plan require me to be indispensable? (Mistake 3.)

Sign of the ego: the goal is to control people or to be obligatory. Sign of self-reliance: the goal is the result, and the plan builds in from the start that I can be replaced.

Step 5. How do I respond to disagreement?

The question: after I was contradicted, what exactly changed, the arguments or the pressure?

Two bad answers. Giving in under pressure: "fine, have it your way," although no arguments were added. Digging in harder: "now I'll definitely do it," although the objection was to the point. In both cases the decision is made by the other person, directly in one case and in reverse in the other.

The good answer: change the decision because of arguments, and do not change it because of pressure.

The mandatory action: say to yourself in words what is new in the objection. If there is nothing new, the decision stands, without a quarrel and without escalation. If there is something new, the decision changes, without a sense of defeat.

Without this action, "firmness" cannot be told from stubbornness.

From Revolver. The final scene: a gun at the temple, the ultimate form of pressure. Jake neither attacks nor pleads. He has received no new arguments, and he does not change his decision. This is the response to pressure without war and without surrender. The elevator scene is built the same way: the voice first threatens, then begs. The same two bad answers, only now they are offered not by an outside person but by the ego itself.

Reading the result

No single step is final by itself.

One step that fires is a reason to look closer.

Two or more mean the ego is making the decision. It must be postponed, and section 7 followed.

A caveat. All the questions here are asked of yourself, and the reactions being checked know how to answer them correctly: "of course I would do it without an audience." The counterweight is not more questions but facts. The first source of facts: what I actually did in similar situations before; for this you need the written record (section 7, item 5). The second: what one person would say who does not care how it turns out. That person is needed as a source of information, not as a judge and not as an audience. The difference shows in what I do with their disagreement (step 5).

From Revolver. Gold's greatest trick is making you believe that he is you. Applied to the check, this means: the voice that says "I've already dealt with my ego, I'm above this" may be that very voice. It may be the one answering the check's questions. That is why the counterweight is the written record and the facts, not confidence in the answers.

7. What to do after the check

If two or more steps fired:

  1. Name it. Determine which of the four mistakes is at work and which voice is speaking (section 3). A named reaction weakens: it stops looking like "just a decision."
  1. Postpone. Decisions with pride mixed in are not made in the same minute. The delay depends on whether it can be undone later: from an hour to several days. What cannot be undone (a breakup, a public statement, quitting a job, refusing a contract) waits at least a day after the first check.
  1. Rephrase it as a task. What result is needed, what it costs, by when. If it cannot be rephrased, there is no task, only a reaction. Which means nothing needs to be done.
  1. Act on the new wording. The feeling may remain; that is normal. The goal is not for the feeling to be gone but for it not to make the decision.
  1. Write it down. What happened, which steps fired, what I did. Reread once a month. Mistakes repeat, and the repetitions are visible only in the record.

From Revolver, on item 1. The elevator scene is exactly "name it." For the first time Jake says to the voice: you are not me. Before that the voice looked like his own thoughts; after that it looks like a voice. Nothing else happens in the elevator, and that turns out to be enough to walk out.

From Revolver, on item 4. After the elevator the voice does not disappear. It keeps talking. The difference is that Jake stops doing what it demands.

An additional technique, from Revolver. Avi and Zach work in one way: they make Jake do what the voice forbids, that is, give, endure, submit. If Avi and Zach are part of Jake himself, this is a method a person applies to himself. In this handbook the technique is used like this: if at step 0 there is no real loss but the resistance to the action is strong, the resistance itself is information. It shows that the action deprives the reaction of its usual food. Resistance without a real loss is not a reason to stop.

8. Five examples

Example 1. An argument in messages

I want to write a long reply and prove the other person wrong.

Step 0: there is no real loss, no money, no business, no obligations. Step 1: if no one saw the reply, I would not write it. Step 2: if this person did not exist, even less so.

Two steps fired. Postpone. It cannot be rephrased as a task: there is no task. The reply is not written.

Example 2. An offer to lead a new project

The anxiety: "what if I fail, how will that look."

Step 0: the loss is real: money, time, the relationship with the client. So part of the question is an ordinary task: work out whether there are enough people and capacity. But the anxiety about "how it will look" is checked separately, through the following steps. Step 1: is the result needed even without an audience? Yes, the work is interesting. Step 3: I measure by the task: what needs to be done and by when. Step 4: the influence is needed for the work; build into the plan from the start who will replace me. Step 5: if the client objects on substance, I will take it into account; if he simply pushes, I will not move.

Nothing fired. Take it on, with the numbers done and a replacement in the plan.

Example 3. A relative asks for money again

Step 0: the loss is real, money. So there is a task: how much can I give without harming myself.

But behind the wish to give there is something else: "if I refuse, I am a bad person, they will stop counting me as one of them." This is mistake 1 (taking on what is not mine) and mistake 3 (being needed).

Divide: mine is deciding the amount and the terms; not mine is how he will use the money and what he will feel after a refusal.

The answer: help within the limits calculated at step 0, without rescuing and without guilt for refusing the rest.

Example 4. Jake Green and Macha's casino (from Revolver)

Jake comes out after seven years in solitary with money and a wish to get even with Macha. He goes to his casino, wins, and humiliates him in front of everyone.

Step 0: there is no real loss from not going. He already has the money. There are no obligations. Step 1: if no one saw Macha humiliated, there would be no point in playing. Step 2: without Macha there is nothing to do in that casino. Step 3: the measure here is Macha's face, not the task. Step 4: what is wanted is not influence for the work but a position on top.

Four steps fired. By the handbook, the decision is postponed and rephrased as a task. No task can be formulated: there is no result needed for its own sake. So, do not go. In the film Jake goes, and gets a contract put on his life. Everything that follows in the film flows from one decision made by the voice.

Example 5. A written poll of the voices before a conversation

I need to tell a business partner that the partnership is ending. The conversation has been put off for three months.

The Frightened One: "He'll be furious, we'll lose clients, everything will fall apart." The Judge: "You should have done this a year ago. Weakling." The Rescuer: "He has a family, he was counting on this, you can't." The Rebel: "Just stop answering. Let him figure it out himself." The Craftsman: "I need time for the work, not for these negotiations." The Observer: "The contract ends in March. The loss is two clients out of nine; that has been counted (step 0). Say it on Tuesday, in person, with a handover plan ready. If he offers different terms on substance, consider them. If he pushes, do not change the decision (step 5)."

The Observer makes the decision. Yet every line has been accounted for: the Frightened One's fear has been turned into a count; the Judge got a date instead of a verdict; the Rescuer was answered by the handover plan; the Rebel is answered by the fact that the conversation will be direct rather than silence; the Craftsman is answered by the date after which time frees up. No voice was suppressed, and no voice made the decision.

9. What this method does not do

It does not give the right decision. It removes bad reasons. After that, the decision still has to be made.

The check itself can become a trap. If the same question is checked many times a day, this is no longer a check but mistake 2 in a new form: comparison with an ideal "correct self." The way out: decide on what there is, and close the question until new information appears.

The conclusion "this is the ego" does not cancel the act. The reaction is mine, and what I do is mine too.

Reactions do not go away with experience. What changes is something else: the time between a reaction and recognizing it gets shorter, and more and more decisions are made not by them.

From Revolver. One of the rules explained to Jake: the more sophisticated the game, the more sophisticated the opponent. The better a person recognizes his reactions, the more reasonable they begin to sound. The check does not become unnecessary with experience; it becomes finer.

10. One-page checklist

  1. What concretely will I lose? If there is a price, it is a task: solve it. If there is no price, go on.
  2. No one will know. Will I still do it?
  3. This person is gone. Will I still do it?
  4. What am I measuring by: the task and my past self, or someone's reaction?
  5. Influence for the work, or for my place? Do I have to be indispensable?
  6. Am I changing because of arguments or because of pressure? What is new in the objection?

Two or more fired: postpone, name, rephrase, act, write down.

Who is speaking right now: the Frightened One, the Judge, the Rescuer, the Rebel, or the Observer? If not the Observer, nothing irreversible.

If the argument sounds like "they will cheat you," "you can't let this stand," "they won't manage without me," "fear me," Gold is speaking.

Appendix. About the film: history, source, limits of use

What was happening with Guy Ritchie

First, what is documented.

After Lock, Stock and Two Smoking Barrels (1998) and Snatch (2000), Ritchie is considered the most successful young director in Britain. In December 2000 he marries Madonna. In 2002 Swept Away comes out with her in the lead: a box-office failure and several Razzies. In those years the British tabloids write about him as "Mr. Madonna."

Both belong to the Kabbalah Centre, an organization that teaches a popular version of Kabbalah. In 2003 they donate 3.5 million pounds toward a building for the London centre.

In August 2003 Sony demands that the Revolver script be rewritten and the Kabbalistic references removed. A studio representative's explanation: "Kabbalah is seen as a cult in some circles. No one wants to be associated with it." Under the threat of the project being shut down, Ritchie agrees, then a few days later fires his agents and shelves the film.

The film is eventually made by Luc Besson: he is co-writer of the story and producer, with a budget of 27 million dollars. The British premiere takes place in September 2005: a gross of 6.7 million, around 15% positive reviews; Roger Ebert (as retold by Vice) suggests firing a gun into the projector rather than showing it.

In 2007 Ritchie re-edits the film for the United States: he simplifies the plot and adds an epilogue in which Deepak Chopra and several PhDs explain directly to the audience that the film is about the ego. In 2008 RocknRolla comes out; the same year he divorces.

Now what is inference rather than fact. There is no direct evidence of a "creative crisis." There are circumstances: public humiliation by his own film, life under constant press scrutiny, a war with the studio over the right to make what he considered important, and at the same time a teaching whose main subject is fear, reaction, and the need to prove. A film about a man run by a voice demanding fear and revenge was made by a man who at that moment had every reason to listen to such a voice. This is consistent with the facts, but it remains an inference.

What he himself thought of the film

In 2008 Ritchie said that nothing went wrong with Revolver: by its very nature it is an esoteric film, not designed for a mass audience, and this is the kind of film he prefers to make.

In the DVD commentary he explained the film's construction: Zach, Avi, and Jake form "a process of three," positive, negative, and the filament between them, with Jake in the position of the central column. The number 32 (chess pieces, floors, vertebrae, teeth) is deliberate. The author's reading therefore matches the one used in this handbook: Avi and Zach are not people but sides of one person.

The 2007 re-edit shows something else as well: the author himself doubted that the film works without an explanation. For the purposes of this handbook the original British version of 2005 is more useful; in it the mechanism is shown, not announced. An explanation in the credits turns an observation the viewer could make on his own into a claim that has to be taken on faith.

The parallels with Kabbalah

First, a distinction without which the conversation makes no sense.

Kabbalah is a mystical tradition within Judaism with centuries of literature (the Zohar, 13th century; Luria, 16th century; Ashlag, 20th century). It is neither a science nor a sect, but a religious tradition.

The Kabbalah Centre is a modern organization founded in the United States by the Berg family. It sells a popular version of this tradition to anyone who wants it: books, courses, objects (red strings, "Kabbalah water"), membership. The accusations of sect-like methods (persistent fundraising, secrecy, followers' dependence on the organization) refer to the Centre, not to the tradition. The Sony representative's words in 2003 were about the Centre.

Ritchie was in the Centre, and the film took specific things from it.

"The Opponent." In the Centre's teaching the ego is called the Opponent: an inner adversary who speaks in the person's own voice and makes him take its desires for his own. Gold is the Opponent. The line about the greatest trick restates this idea.

Reaction and "restriction." The Centre teaches that almost all human actions are reactions, and the only action that truly belongs to a person is stopping the reaction before it becomes an act. In the handbook this is "postpone" (section 7, item 2); in the film it happens in the elevator.

"The formula." The Centre's popular books contain a "proactive formula" of several steps: an obstacle; realize that the real opponent is not the obstacle but your own reaction; stop the reaction; act. "The formula" in the film carries the same name and is built the same way.

The three columns. The right, left, and central columns of the Kabbalistic scheme stand for giving, receiving, and the balance between them. Ritchie says outright that Zach, Avi, and Jake are these.

The number 32 refers to the "32 paths of wisdom" from the Book of Creation: ten sefirot and twenty-two letters.

How productive this is for the handbook

The observation is productive; the teaching is not. The difference can be tested.

What works without the Centre. The idea of an inner adversary who speaks in your voice is found in the Stoics (Epictetus: it is not things that disturb us but our judgments about things), in the Buddhist practice of observing arising thoughts, in cognitive therapy (Beck's automatic thoughts), in Jung (the shadow), in Berne (parent, adult, child), in Schwartz's work with "parts." The pause between impulse and act is in every one of these traditions. The Centre did not discover it and does not own it. The handbook uses only this, and it needs not a single word from the Centre's vocabulary.

What does not work. Everything that cannot be tested against behavior: the Light, the "99 percent reality," numbers, objects, water. The tests in section 6 can be checked within a week against your own actions; the numbers 32 and 72 cannot be checked at all. For the handbook they are useless.

What is harmful, specifically for the purposes of this handbook.

The teaching calls the ego an enemy to be defeated. The handbook starts from the opposite: the ego is a part of a person with a useful function, and a war with it is a war with yourself, the same argument between two voices that has no end (section 3). Here the film is closer to the handbook than to its source: in the elevator Jake does not defeat the voice; he stops obeying it.

The teaching gives the ego a new place to hide. "I am proactive," "I am in the Light," "I have restricted my Opponent": this is a feeling of superiority over those who are "reactive." The same Gold, only harder to notice: he speaks in the words of the teaching about fighting him.

The teaching ties you to an organization. Courses, a mentor, membership, donations: a method that works only inside the organization is not a tool but a tie. By section 5 this is the opposite of self-reliance: no single tie should carry everything, including the one that promises liberation. The hot tub scene in section 5 shows the opposite: a teacher who refuses to be needed.

Three questions for any teaching, wherever it comes from. Can it be tested against my own behavior within a week? Does it work without the organization, the mentor, and their vocabulary? Does it make a person more independent or more attached? What passes all three can be taken. The rest need not be.

In sum. The film is more useful than its source because it shows the mechanism instead of preaching it: to see Gold you do not have to believe in anything; it is enough to remember the elevator scene. Ritchie took an observation from the Centre, expressed it in the genre he commanded, and produced something that works regardless of what one thinks of the Centre itself. The handbook does the same: it takes the observation and leaves the teaching.
